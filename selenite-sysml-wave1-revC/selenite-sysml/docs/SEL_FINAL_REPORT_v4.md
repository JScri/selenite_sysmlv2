## Part 1: The Solution to the House of No Waste Competition — The Selenite Programme

### Selenite: A lunar house of no waste for Earth's rarest resources

Rare earth elements (REEs) underpin the clean energy transition: wind turbines, electric vehicle motors, LED lighting, and MRI magnets all depend on REEs. The mining that supplies them, however, is amongst the most environmentally destructive industries on Earth. REE extraction consumes over 120 million tonnes of chemical reagent per year, contaminates groundwater with radioactive thorium and uranium tailings, strips topsoil across thousands of square kilometres, and generates roughly 30 tonnes of CO2-equivalent per tonne of refined oxide (Lee & Wen, 2017). Every solar panel and electric vehicle that reduces carbon emissions depends on a supply chain that poisons the land it touches, a cost inherited by every generation that follows.

The Selenite Programme proposes a structural solution: move REE extraction off Earth entirely. Build a permanent crewed base at Shackleton Crater in the South Pole-Aitken basin that proves autonomous mining and zero-carbon processing chemistry. Then deploy that proven flowsheet at industrial scale across a purpose-built, fully autonomous factory at the Procellarum KREEP Terrane (while SPA specialises as the programme's asteroid processing hub, water-ice ISRU centre and human remote control of systems at PKT). Over 200 years, lunar-sourced rare earth oxide production scales to 2.5 million tonnes per year: full replacement of terrestrial REE mining.

### The House: A closed-loop lunar habitat

The crewed facility at Shackleton Crater rim is, by necessity, a building that wastes nothing. Resupply from Earth costs thousands of dollars per kilogram, and every gram lost from the loop must be launched from the bottom of a 9.4 km/s gravity well, necessitating circularity.

Eight regolith-sheltered pressure modules branch from a central Operations Hub in three functional arms: habitation, food production, and EVA access. Modules sit in shallow cradles, buried under 2.0-2.5 metres of lunar regolith beneath a sintered structural shell, built entirely from local material by autonomous construction robots. The shielding mass alone is ~1.5 million kg, with zero imported insulation, concrete or steel framing.

Inside, the Environmental Control and Life Support System (ECLSS) recovers 93-98% of all water (Williamson et al., 2023) through gravity-adapted distillation, replacing failure-prone micro-gravity centrifuges and cutting hardware mass (Anderson et al., 2018). Every molecule of crew-exhaled CO2 is consumed: 59% feeds a Sabatier reactor to recover water and oxygen, 41% feeds a three-tier aeroponic greenhouse that produces food and supplementary oxygen. Crew waste nutrients cycle through a nitrification bioreactor back into the greenhouse. Total annual resupply from Earth is roughly 3.4 t for a crew of four (scales up as the hab grows to accommodate 20 people).

### The Factory: Zero-waste processing and self-replicating industry

The programme's eight-step hydrometallurgical flowsheet eliminates D2EHPA organic solvent entirely, the most toxic reagent in terrestrial REE processing. Sulphuric acid is produced in situ from lunar troilite material. Calcium hydroxide replaces imported sodium hydroxide, synthesised from lunar rock. Thorium hydroxide, the radioactive byproduct that contaminates terrestrial tailings ponds, becomes fuel for a fleet of molten-salt reactors (MSRs) that power the autonomous factory at PKT. As SPA scales, MSRs are employed at the first base too, to accommodate asteroid processing demands. The moon has no groundwater to contaminate, no biosphere to damage, no atmosphere to pollute. Processing waste (inert calcium phosphate and silica) is redeposited on the surface.

From Phase 9, a lunar foundry manufactures its own expansion hardware from local iron, nickel, aluminium and titanium, producing mining haulers at 57% in-situ content initially, scaling toward full self-replication. The factory builds the machines that build the factory. PKT circuit commissioning is phased with the retirement of SPA's chemical hopper fleet, redirecting water production from propellant to process supply via mass driver. Earth reagent dependency drops from 122 million tonnes per year to approximately 6000 (a reduction of 99.995%).

### Beyond REE: Iridium, hydrogen and Earth independence

The programme's environmental impact extends beyond rare earths. Captured M-type asteroids yield platinum group metals including iridium, the critical bottleneck material for PEM electrolysers that underpin the hydrogen fuel economy (Minke et al., 2021). At steady state, asteroid-sourced iridium enables 107 million tonnes of CO2 avoidance per year through accelerated hydrogen adoption (International Energy Agency, 2024), more than the 75 million tonnes avoided by displacing REE mining itself. Diversified asteroid captures progressively close every remaining Earth dependency: M-type iron-nickel for structural manufacturing, C-type water and organics for propellant independence, S-type silicon and copper for in-situ electronics. Each asteroid type enables the next. Earth mass dependency eventually falls below 0.5%.

### The value chain: from one-directional to circular

Material flow evolves through three eras. In the Foundation Era (Y1-25), flow is one-directional: Earth exports all capital to the Moon. Every system that must later work autonomously is proved at Shackleton with close crew oversight. In the Scaling Era, a second site activates with zero permanent crew, connected by electromagnetic mass drivers at near-zero marginal cost. Flow becomes bi-directional: REE oxides return to Earth. In the Independence Era, diversified asteroid captures and lunar manufacturing close the loop entirely. The system sustains and expands itself from space-sourced materials.

### Why this matters

The programme costs $21.3 trillion over 200 years, modelled bottom-up from unit specifications using mature heavy-lift launch costs projected for the mid-2030s (SpaceX initial 2028 pricing: $100k/kg; model assumes mature-fleet reduction). Environmental externalities, valued using the EPA's Social Cost of Carbon at $190/t CO2 (2023) and economy-wide input-output damage assessment per Wei et al. (2022) account for the majority of programme benefits. Lunar-sourced REO is priced below terrestrial production cost by design: the programme operates in public utility, not a profit-maximising entity, deliberately driving mine closure through price displacement.

The benefit-cost ratio of 0.50 over the full horizon reflects a 40-year capital-intensive build phase; at annual steady state, BCR reaches 2.06 with a net surplus of $121.8 billion per year. The programme's 2.5 Mt/y target exceeds all current demand projections; if demand grows beyond this level, the modular architecture supports additional scaling using the same infrastructure design. The dominant sensitivity is discount rate (Arrow et al., 2014), confirming that the economic case is fundamentally an intergenerational ethics question: a commitment by one generation to permanently remove a destructive industry from the planet for every generation that follows.

The Selenite habitat is not a metaphor for circularity. It is a building where nothing is wasted because nothing can be.
